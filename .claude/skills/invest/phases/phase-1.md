# Phase 1 — Pricing-power architecture

**The question:** who sets this company's prices, and what can override that?

Everything downstream depends on the answer. A company that cannot raise price to cover cost is not a growth story regardless of its volumes.

## 1.1 The price-setting mechanism

Establish, with statutory or contractual citations, exactly how the realised price per unit is determined.

**Regulated businesses** — the mechanism is law, so read the law:
- The relationship with each regulator and ministry. Who approves tariffs, who approves capex, who appoints the board, and where those powers conflict.
- The **cost pass-through formula**: which costs pass through, on what lag, with what true-up, and whether the pass-through is automatic or discretionary.
- **The political override.** The single most important finding in this phase. Under what exact legal conditions can a government suspend, defer or reverse an approved increase? Has it done so? What did that cost equity holders? Distinguish what the statute permits from what the political reality has been — where they diverge, the reality is the binding constraint.
- **Wholesale price caps or compensation mechanisms** that limit input cost during commodity spikes: the legal thresholds, who bears the residual, and how much downside protection this actually provides.

**Competitive businesses** — the mechanism is structure:
- Market share and concentration; the realistic threat of share loss on a price increase.
- Switching costs, contract length, and repricing points.
- Input-cost pass-through: historical evidence of how much and how fast, not management's claim about it.
- The binding statutes here are usually antitrust, export control, sanctions, data and safety regimes. Treat them with the same rigour as a tariff order.

## 1.2 Geographic and segment price differentiation

- Where prices differ by region, customer class or channel, map the differentials and the direction of travel.
- Quantify the **net** revenue impact of any announced differentiation. A cut in one region and a premium in another is not neutral — model both legs and net them.
- Where reform changes procurement cost as well as sales price, model both sides. Reforms are frequently sold on the revenue line and paid for on the cost line.

## 1.3 Ownership, governance and shareholder alignment

- Ownership structure: state, founder, family, cross-holdings, free float. Identify who actually controls the outcome.
- Does a controlling owner have objectives that conflict with minority shareholders? For a state-controlled monopoly, industrial competitiveness and low consumer prices routinely outrank shareholder returns — state this plainly and quantify what it has cost.
- Participation in any governance-reform or value-up programme: is it real, and is there a mechanism that forces follow-through?
- Capital return policy: dividend, buyback, and whether the controlling owner benefits from them.
- **The structural discount.** Determine whether this company trades at a persistent discount to peers for governance, jurisdiction or ownership reasons, and whether anything in the current setup dissolves it. A discount that has persisted for a decade needs a specific catalyst to close, not a hope.

## Required output

- A table of the price-setting mechanism: input → mechanism → lag → who bears the residual.
- A table of override events: date, action, trigger, equity impact.
- Ownership table with the alignment assessment.
- A written answer to: **can this company raise price to cover cost, and who decides?**
