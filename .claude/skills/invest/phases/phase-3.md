# Phase 3 — Demand & monetisation structure

**The question:** where does incremental demand come from, how firm is it, and does serving it create value?

This is where the growth-≠-value guardrail does its hardest work. Most sell-side enthusiasm dies here, correctly.

## 3.1 Quantify the demand

- Map named demand sources through a stated horizon. Name the counterparties. Aggregate demand forecasts are worth little; named projects with named sponsors are worth a great deal.
- Convert every commitment into the units that drive revenue — peak capacity, annual volume, unit shipments, subscribers, square metres — and show the conversion arithmetic and its assumptions (utilisation, load factor, ramp).
- Build the demand bridge: current volume → additions by source → attrition and efficiency losses → forecast volume. Efficiency and substitution are real negative terms; a bridge without them is a sales document.

## 3.2 Classify every opportunity

Every demand item gets exactly one classification. This single discipline separates a real pipeline from a press release:

| Class | Test |
|---|---|
| `[Existing Contracted]` | Signed, binding, with take-or-pay or equivalent. Revenue is a near-certainty. |
| `[Approved / Committed Pipeline]` | Approved interconnection, permit granted, FID taken, capacity reserved. Highly likely, timing uncertain. |
| `[Unfunded Planned Project]` | Announced by a credible sponsor without financing or final approval. Genuinely uncertain. |
| `[Speculative Demand]` | Analyst extrapolation, MOU, or a government aspiration. Zero weight in the base case. |

State the volume in each bucket. Only the first two belong in a base case; the third belongs in the bull case; the fourth belongs nowhere but the narrative.

## 3.3 Value capture — is the volume worth having?

Establish the price and margin at which incremental demand is actually served:

- Which tariff class, contract or price list applies? Standard terms usually mean standard margins — large new customers frequently arrive on the *lowest* unit price in the book because of their volume.
- What are the alternative commercial structures, and are they in use here?
  - **Upfront contributions to infrastructure cost** (customer capital contributions, connection charges) — these change the return profile fundamentally by shifting capex to the customer.
  - **Direct bilateral contracts and wheeling or access fees.**
  - **Premium service tiers** — reliability, dedicated capacity, priority access.
  - Long-term contracts with indexation, take-or-pay, or minimum-volume floors.
- **Incremental margin, not average margin.** Compute the contribution margin on the marginal unit after the cost to serve it, including the capital required. Compare it to the company's current average. If incremental margin is below average margin, growth dilutes returns — say so in exactly those words.
- Cost to serve: what capex must be built to serve this demand, and does the pricing recover it within the asset life?

## Required output

- Demand table: source, counterparty, capacity, annual volume, start year, classification.
- Volume totals by classification bucket.
- Demand bridge from current to forecast volume.
- Commercial structure table: mechanism, in use (yes/no), economics.
- The incremental-margin calculation, shown explicitly.
- An unambiguous written answer: **does this demand create value, destroy it, or merely relocate it?**

Contribute volume and revenue trajectories to `model.fcf` scenarios, and flag any demand class that only appears in the bull case.
